# Reading 1 — Spring is won in the soil

Date: 2026-10-04
Session type: Reading
Next session: Test on 2026-10-05

## Reading

Spring can make a productive garden feel full of possibility. Seedlings are appearing in nurseries, the days are getting longer, and it is tempting to start planting immediately. But a successful summer harvest often depends on what happens before the first tomato or basil plant goes into the ground.

One of the most useful jobs in early spring is to rebuild the soil after winter crops. Vegetables remove nutrients as they grow, and repeated harvesting can gradually leave a bed depleted. Rather than relying only on packaged fertiliser, gardeners can return organic matter to the soil with compost, worm castings and aged manure. These materials do more than feed plants: they also improve soil structure and help it retain moisture.

Recent Australian gardening advice recommends using cured compost in spring. Cured compost has already broken down and is stable enough to spread around plants without generating too much heat. A layer of compost across the soil surface can be followed by mulch, such as straw. The compost adds nutrients and microorganisms, while the mulch protects the surface from sun and wind and slows water loss.

This is especially useful in productive gardens because vegetables are demanding plants. A lettuce that is harvested after a few weeks and a tomato plant that produces fruit for months have different needs, but both depend on healthy soil. Gardeners also have to think about timing. If warm-season seedlings are planted too early, cold nights can slow their growth. If they are planted too late, part of the growing season is lost.

A productive garden is therefore less about forcing plants to grow quickly and more about creating the conditions in which they can grow well. Good preparation includes checking the soil, adding organic matter, protecting moisture with mulch and choosing crops that suit the season. It may feel slower at the beginning, but careful preparation often saves work later.

There is also a useful lesson here for small gardens. You do not need a huge backyard to improve productivity. A raised bed, balcony planter or rooftop garden can use the same principles. The scale changes, but the basic system remains the same: feed the soil, protect it, observe the weather and plant at the right time.
